## Effect of Pulsatile Flow Waveform and Womersley Number on the Flow in Stenosed Arterial Geometry

“We have expressed the pulsatility in the flow through a variation in Reynolds number [14, 45] around a mean Reynolds number (Remean) of 200. The Reynolds number (Re = ρud/μ) is defined on the basis of the diameter of the unstenosed artery, time-averaged mean velocity of flow, and the density and viscosity of blood, which are taken as ρ = 1056 Kg/m3 and μ = .00345 PaS, respectively.” ([Banerjee et al., 2012, p. 3](zotero://select/library/items/4Y6P5EP8)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/PI3BCRP7?page=3&annotation=TJ3T85XQ))

“Following the recommendations of Caro et al. [43] and Tutty [44], we adopted three different values of α as 4, 7.5, and 12.5 following different physiological conditions of the body. In a flow distribution network that progresses from a large tube to many small tubes (e.g., a blood vessel network), the Womersley number is also changing progressively from a higher value to the lower value. The Womersley numbers tend to 1 at the level of the terminal arteries. In the arterioles, capillaries, and venules, the Womersley numbers are less than one. In these regions the inertia force becomes less important, and the flow is determined by the balance of viscous stresses and the pressure gradient.” ([Banerjee et al., 2012, p. 4](zotero://select/library/items/4Y6P5EP8)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/PI3BCRP7?page=4&annotation=9R866BDT))

## Revisiting Murray’s Law in Pulmonary Arteries: Exploring branching patterns and principles

“This study investigates the morphometry of pulmonary arterial networks, examining the relationships between parent and child vessel diameters across different species. We analyzed three-dimensional segmentations of pulmonary arterial geometries from healthy adult subjects across four species: human (n=7), canine (n=5), swine (n=4), and murine (n=3). Our findings reveal an average exponent value of 2.31 (± 0.60) in humans, 2.13 (± 0.54) in canine, 2.10 (± 0.49) in swine and 2.59 (± 0.58) in murine, all lower than the predicted value of 3.0 from Murray’s Law. Extending Murray’s Law to consider fully-developed pulsatile flow based on minimal impedance, we show that mean flow is proportional to radius raised to a power between 2.1 and 3, depending on the Womersley number. In summary, our findings suggest that while the traditional Murray’s Law provides a useful baseline, pulmonary artery branching follows a different optimization principle that appears to depend on Womersley number as well as other, unknown factors.” ([Correa et al., 2025, p. 1](zotero://select/library/items/2BSDT8M6)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/M2HJAFQX?page=1&annotation=UXJX47U9))

“The Womersley number (W o) is a dimensionless parameter that characterizes the unsteadiness of pulsatile flow in relation to viscous effects.” ([Correa et al., 2025, p. 7](zotero://select/library/items/2BSDT8M6)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/M2HJAFQX?page=7&annotation=J33RIPQC))

“Averaging across all bifurcations in all species, the mean exponent was 2.43 ± 0.32 (mean ± SD), which was significantly lower than the theoretical Murray value (p < 0.0001).” ([Correa et al., 2025, p. 8](zotero://select/library/items/2BSDT8M6)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/M2HJAFQX?page=8&annotation=DPIJRUBJ))

“However, when the assumption of fully-developed laminar flow is relaxed to include a range of flow profiles from parabolic to blunt, this exponent varies between 2.3 and 3. “The abnormal vascular structure leads to spatially and temporally heterogeneous blood perfusion in tumors.31 In any given vessel, blood flow can change with time. “Such a problem was posed by Thomas Young in his Croonian Lecture in 1808 (Young, 1809) when he wished to estimate the resistance of an arterial system: "In order to calculate the magnitude of the resistance, it is necessary to determine the dimensions of the arterial system, and the velocity of the blood which flows through it."
